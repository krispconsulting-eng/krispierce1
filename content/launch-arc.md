# Kris Pierce Consulting: Launch Arc

A finite, four-beat introductory campaign that introduces the consulting practice
across LinkedIn, X, Instagram, and Substack. Runs over roughly three weeks, then
hands over to always-on positioning so the feed stops sounding like an announcement.

Built with the `kris-social-positioning` skill (launch mode); voice governed by
`kris-pierce-consulting`. One idea per post, peer to peer, Code-safe, no em dashes.
Leads with you and the work, reserving "I" for emphasis (the live-site rule).

---

## Beat 1 - The why (the founding idea)

**Hero message:** Most health decisions are still made about patients and then
explained to them. Closing that gap is the reason the practice exists.

### Substack (anchor piece, publish first)

**Title:** Made about them, explained to them
**Subtitle:** Why a consulting practice, instead of another engagement framework

> There is a meeting I think about often. A very good team, a very good plan, solid evidence, genuine intent. And not one person affected by the decision was in the room while it was being shaped. They would be told about it later, in a newsletter, once it was already built.
>
> That meeting was not unusual. It is the default. Across health services, research, and rare disease, the pattern repeats: decisions get made about people, then explained to them. Consultation gets added near the end, when the design is already locked, and everyone calls it engagement.
>
> Both sides of that gap look different up close. As a carer in rare disease, being told about a decision after it was made is a familiar feeling. From senior advisory roles in health technology assessment, genomics, and consumer engagement, the view from inside the room is just as familiar. Each side explains the other. The people in the room are not careless. They are busy, accountable, and working inside systems that make late, shallow consultation the path of least resistance.
>
> So the problem is not intent. It is method. Frameworks telling organisations they should engage are everywhere. Far rarer is the method that shows how to do it so the engagement actually changes the decision, rather than decorating it.
>
> That gap is what the practice exists to close. Not to give anyone a voice, a phrase worth distrusting, because people already have voices. The work is to change who is in the room while the decision is still moving, and to build the methods that make that practical rather than performative.
>
> The next couple of weeks will lay out how that works: what real partnership looks like next to the box-ticking version, who it helps, and why it pays off for them. This first piece is just the conviction underneath all of it.
>
> Decisions made with the people most affected are better decisions. Not kinder. Better. That is the whole argument, and the rest is method.

## Beat 2 - The what (how she works)

**Hero message:** Consultation asks people what they think after the design is locked.
Partnership builds with them while it can still change. That difference is the whole method.

### LinkedIn

> Consultation is asking people what they think after the design is locked. Partnership is building with them while it can still change.
>
> The two look similar from the outside. Both produce quotes, slides, and a sense that community was involved. Only one of them changes the decision.
>
> A concrete version. A team designing a study can run a survey at the end and report that patients were consulted. Or they can bring patients and carers in at the question-setting stage, where what gets measured is still open. The first produces a tick. The second changes what the study finds, because the people who live with the condition know which outcomes actually matter and which the protocol was about to miss.
>
> Same people. Same goodwill. Completely different result, decided entirely by when they were in the room.
>
> Most of the work is moving that moment earlier, and building the methods that make early partnership practical rather than a nice idea everyone runs out of time for.
>
> No fixed method. The method follows the question. The point is never the method; it is what changes because of it.

## Beat 3 - The who (who she helps and why it pays off)

**Hero message:** Earlier, genuine partnership pays off differently for each group:
stronger evidence for health organisations, lower risk and more trust for industry,
and a stronger mission for not-for-profits.

### LinkedIn

> Moving partnership earlier pays off, but it pays off differently depending on who you are. Three audiences, three reasons.
>
> For health organisations and research teams, it is better evidence. Participatory research builds patient and carer experience into the question, not just the write-up, so the findings hold up and the decisions they feed are sounder.
>
> For industry partners in pharmaceutical and MedTech, it is risk and trust. Authentic patient and carer insight, gathered properly and early, surfaces what people actually need before, during, and after a launch, rather than after an expensive assumption has already been made. The Medicines Australia Code shapes this work; done well, engagement meets compliance as a byproduct, not the other way around.
>
> For not-for-profits and advocacy groups, it is mission. Real partnership with the community you exist to serve carries weight with members, funders, and the bodies that make decisions about them. It is the difference between speaking for people and working with them.
>
> Different payoffs, same underlying method: bring the people most affected in while the decision can still move. It is just worth different things to different people.
>
> Which of these is closest to your work?
